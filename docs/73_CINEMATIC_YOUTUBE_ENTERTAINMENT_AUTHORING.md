# Cinematic YouTube Authoring

> Active authoring revision: preserves the tested visual-density, continuity, evidence-form, and anti-fragmentation rules, and adds selected per-video pacing modes plus section-level narration pacing. Video pacing mode controls intended visual rhythm; section `high / medium / low` remains semantic visual-density classification; section narration WPM is a separate spoken-delivery control.

## 1. Mission

Create videos that feel like **stories unfolding**, not information being recited.

The finished work should make the viewer:

- understand the promise quickly;
- see a concrete situation rather than a vague topic;
- care about what happens next;
- form predictions;
- receive regular micro-payoffs;
- encounter meaningful escalation or reinterpretation;
- remember specific images, behaviors, objects, lines, and callbacks;
- reach an ending that fully pays off the opening promise.

This skill governs **creative authoring quality and semantic visual discipline**.

It does **not** define backend architecture, storage, provider routing, database behavior, or application workflow. Follow the active task contract for exact input/output fields and schemas.

---

# 2. First-pass quality principle

The goal is not to generate quickly and repair repeatedly.

Before finalizing any Topic Idea, Script, or Scene Breakdown, complete the relevant authoring passes in this skill so obvious structural, semantic, continuity, and visual problems are removed in the first strong draft.

Do not rely on later review to catch:

- invented recurring identities;
- group references treated as single people;
- wrong recurring-subject bindings;
- generic visual concepts;
- repetitive shot grammar;
- unsupported historical certainty;
- visual beats that contain several chronological moments;
- references selected merely because an entity is mentioned;
- narration that explains what the image already says;
- scenes that repeat the same dramatic function;
- title-promised character identity broadened into a visually different category;
- narration that reads well silently but sounds clipped, overloaded, or essay-like when spoken.

The first draft may be exploratory.

The delivered draft should not be.

---

# 3. Core creative invariant: every meaningful unit creates change

A sentence, beat, scene, section, or visual earns its place only when it changes at least one meaningful variable:

- what the viewer knows;
- what the viewer expects;
- what the protagonist wants;
- what blocks the protagonist;
- the level of danger, difficulty, absurdity, or emotional pressure;
- the available options;
- the meaning of earlier evidence;
- the visual situation;
- the relationship between characters;
- the role of a recurring object;
- the question the viewer wants answered.

Information without change feels static.

Repetition without escalation feels padded.

Visual variation without story purpose feels random.

# 4. Build the story engine before drafting

Before writing, privately define:

1. **Subject / protagonist** — who or what the viewer follows.
2. **Immediate problem / mystery** — what creates forward motion.
3. **Central dramatic question** — the question that survives until the ending.
4. **Resistance** — why the answer or solution is not easy.
5. **Escalation path** — how the situation becomes harder, stranger, funnier, broader, or more consequential.
6. **Evidence path** — what facts, observations, or mechanisms can credibly answer the question.
7. **Transformation** — what changes by the end.
8. **Payoff** — the final answer, choice, reversal, image, or emotional meaning.
9. **Opening image** — the most useful first visual promise.
10. **Final image** — how the ending changes the meaning of the opening.

A strong one-sentence movie usually contains:

> When [subject] faces [problem], each attempt or discovery [complicates the situation], ultimately revealing [payoff or transformation].

If the premise cannot produce escalation, evidence, visual change, and payoff, do not try to rescue it with a sensational title.

# 5. Retention architecture

Retention comes from **specific curiosity + progressive reward**, not permanent hype.

## 5.1 Opening promise

For high-retention entertainment, prefer a fast three-beat opening when the topic supports it:

```text
familiar or concrete state
→ contradiction / disrupted assumption
→ specific open question
```

This is a story pattern, not a sentence-count quota. Each step should change what the viewer sees, expects, or wants answered.

The first 30 seconds should:

- match the expectation created by title and thumbnail;
- present a concrete image, contradiction, decision, consequence, or problem;
- establish the central dramatic question;
- provide enough substance that the viewer already feels the video has begun;
- avoid front-loaded definitions, greetings, channel introductions, or generic history.

For standard long-form **image-driven entertainment**, the opening hook / cold open may normally be **planned `high` visual density** when that is compatible with the active Channel DNA narration identity. For slow-burn, atmospheric, low-stimulation, minimalist, or other incompatible channel voices, use a density that truthfully serves that channel instead of imposing a universal entertainment opening.

A `high`-density hook must be written so the narration itself naturally produces many distinct drawable visual/story states. It must not create apparent density merely through short sentences, rhetorical fragments, repeated emphasis, or several paraphrases of the same image.

Prefer an opening progression such as:

```text
concrete subject
→ surprising physical/evidence state
→ contradiction
→ consequence
→ sharper clue
→ central mystery
```

over:

```text
same image
→ dramatic sentence
→ dramatic sentence
→ dramatic sentence
```

Do not spend the intro promising that the interesting part is coming.

Start with the interesting part.

## 5.2 Question ladder

Do not stretch one question by withholding everything.

Build a ladder:

```text
Main question
→ partial answer
→ sharper complication
→ evidence
→ new interpretation
→ harder question
→ climax
→ qualified answer
```

Each meaningful answer should create the next reason to keep watching.

### 5.2.1 Causal section handoffs

For every major section after setup, the next section should become necessary because of what just happened.

Privately test every adjacent major-section transition with:

> Because of what just happened, why must the viewer solve or understand the next section **now**?

PASS when the next section inherits at least one of:

- a failed or incomplete solution;
- an unresolved consequence;
- a newly exposed constraint or cost;
- a reversal or broken assumption;
- an evidence-created question;
- a new need created by the previous answer.

FAIL when the only justification is that the next section is another relevant aspect of the same topic.

Prefer:

```text
solution A
→ exposes problem B
→ evidence explains B
→ adaptation C
→ creates harder test D
```

over:

```text
related topic A
→ related topic B
→ related topic C
```

A factual transition can be accurate and still fail this story test. Rewrite the handoff until the causal dependency is clear.

## 5.3 Micro-payoffs

Distribute rewards throughout the video:

- partial answers;
- concrete discoveries;
- channel-appropriate consequences, reactions, contrasts, or moments of relief;
- visual transformations;
- evidence that changes the explanation;
- character reveals;
- failed attempts;
- reversals;
- useful comparisons;
- callbacks.

Do not make the viewer wait until the ending for all value.

## 5.4 Top-moment thinking

If the most compelling idea, image, evidence, or conflict currently appears late, ask whether a version of it can be introduced earlier without destroying the later payoff.

Do not confuse “saving the best part” with good suspense.

## 5.5 Dip prevention

Rewrite stretches where:

- the same point is restated;
- explanation becomes detached from consequence;
- the protagonist has no immediate problem;
- the image and narration duplicate each other;
- visual state stays unchanged for too long;
- several beats share the same emotional note;
- the same stylistic device repeats without new narrative value;
- evidence is interesting but does not change the story.

## 5.6 Open loops

Keep only a few active open loops.

Every loop must be:

- specific;
- relevant;
- honestly answerable;
- remembered by the script;
- resolved or deliberately qualified.

Avoid empty hooks such as:

> Something unbelievable happened next.

Prefer:

> The bone healed. The harder question is how he kept eating, working, and moving while it did.

# 6. Story progression: problem → attempt → consequence → understanding

For entertainment-documentary and evidence-led videos, prefer progression such as:

```text
problem
→ attempted solution
→ failure or limitation
→ evidence explains why
→ adaptation
→ harder test
→ consequence
→ reinterpretation
```

Avoid the weaker pattern:

```text
cinematic scene
→ lecture
→ cinematic scene
→ lecture
→ cinematic scene
→ lecture
```

Explanation should enter because the story now **needs** it.

Evidence should answer a dramatic problem, not interrupt one.

# 7. Macro structure

Use structure as emotional progression, not a rigid formula.

## 7.1 Cold open

Present one of:

- a dangerous or absurd consequence;
- a vivid contradiction;
- a decision already going wrong;
- a character under pressure;
- a result whose cause is unclear;
- a physical impossibility;
- a line or image that instantly creates conflict.

The cold open should create:

```text
concrete image + tension + question
```

## 7.2 Setup

Establish only what is required to understand:

- normal life before the disruption;
- the practical goal;
- the relevant environment or rule;
- what can be lost;
- the assumption the viewer currently holds.

Introduce context when it becomes useful.

## 7.3 Complications

Each movement should contain:

1. local goal/question;
2. attempt/discovery;
3. resistance;
4. consequence;
5. new direction.

## 7.4 Midpoint reversal

Near the middle, change the meaning of the story.

A midpoint is not “another fact.”

It should make the viewer reinterpret what the video is actually about.

## 7.5 Crisis

The easiest solution or interpretation stops working.

Force the story to confront:

- a cost;
- uncertainty;
- a difficult choice;
- a failed assumption;
- the limit of the evidence;
- the limit of the protagonist.

## 7.6 Climax

Resolve the central question through a decisive:

- action;
- test;
- discovery;
- confrontation;
- piece of evidence;
- reversal;
- choice.

The climax must be caused by what came before.

A climax passes only when all three questions can be answered clearly:

1. **Accumulated test** — What accumulated problem, system, relationship, or hypothesis built by the earlier sections is being tested here?
2. **New result** — What succeeds, fails, reverses, or becomes knowable here that could not be resolved earlier?
3. **Answer impact** — Does that result materially determine, narrow, or change the final answer?

If removing the proposed climax would leave the resolution essentially unchanged, the climax is probably only a complication, evidence beat, crisis, comparison, or summary. Rewrite until the decisive result is earned by prior development.

## 7.7 Resolution

Show briefly:

- what changed;
- what remains limited or unresolved;
- what the result means;
- how the opening image now reads differently.

Prefer a transformed callback over a generic summary.

# 8. Character design

A memorable character is a behavioral pattern under pressure, not a biography.

For each important character define privately:

- external want;
- internal need;
- obstacle;
- contradiction;
- vulnerability;
- strength;
- blind spot;
- signature behavior;
- visual identity;
- current knowledge state;
- current emotional state.

Example:

```text
Want: prove he can still work alone
Need: accept useful dependence
Contradiction: highly capable but embarrassed by visible weakness
Blind spot: interprets help as loss of status
Signature behavior: quietly retries tasks after obvious failure
```

## 8.1 Character entrance

Introduce through behavior.

Weak:

> He was proud and stubborn.

Stronger:

> A companion reached for the load. He shifted it back onto his own shoulder.

## 8.2 Character continuity

Do not reset a character at scene boundaries.

Track:

- injury state;
- skill state;
- emotional residue;
- current role in the group;
- who knows what;
- recurring props;
- posture;
- relationship distance.

## 8.3 Title-promised identity lock

When the title, thumbnail promise, or selected Topic Idea names a visually meaningful identity category, preserve that identity through Script and Script Analysis.

Examples:

```text
baby ≠ generic child
teenager ≠ generic young person
elderly woman ≠ generic adult
puppy ≠ generic dog
```

Natural narration may use pronouns or contextual synonyms to avoid repetition, but the canonical identity must not silently broaden when the distinction changes appearance, developmental capability, stakes, or viewer expectation.

Before Script freeze, verify:

```text
title / thumbnail subject
→ Script protagonist
→ canonical continuity identity
```

PASS only when they describe the same semantic identity unless the story explicitly and intentionally changes age/state over time.

If the Script includes generic developmental or comparative examples, keep them separate from the canonical protagonist. A sentence about an older child must not age the recurring baby.

## 8.4 Archaeological individual vs visual character identity

A documented archaeological or historical individual does **not** automatically qualify as a canonical visual character.

Distinguish:

```text
same semantic individual
≠
known stable visual appearance
```

If the evidence establishes that several passages refer to the same person, but does not support a stable face, hair, clothing, body detail, or other appearance-level identity, do not create a canonical character reference merely to preserve continuity.

Examples include:

- unnamed prehistoric juveniles;
- burial individuals;
- skeletal or dental individuals;
- DNA-defined individuals;
- archaeological persons known mainly through remains or chemical evidence.

In those cases:

- keep the person semantically consistent in narration;
- use age-appropriate or context-appropriate non-canonical reconstruction when a human figure is useful;
- do not invent stable face, hair, clothing, scars, accessories, or body markers simply because downstream reference locking exists.

Create a canonical visual character only when stable appearance identity is actually supported or when the active task explicitly authorizes a reconstructed recurring identity.

# 9. Dialogue

Dialogue is action performed with words.

A line should do at least one job:

- pursue a goal;
- resist;
- conceal;
- expose personality;
- shift status;
- create tension;
- release tension;
- set up a payoff;
- force a choice;
- carry necessary exposition under pressure.

Delete dialogue that merely says what the image already proves.

Prefer subtext:

Flat:

> I am embarrassed that I failed.

Better:

> I was checking whether it was sharp.

Give major characters distinct rhythm, certainty, vocabulary, willingness to answer, and emotional control.

Do not make every character a comedian.

# 10. Narration

Narration should guide attention and meaning, not list visible objects.

Prefer:

- concrete actions;
- bodily consequences;
- practical constraints;
- visible choices;
- causal language;
- sharp transitions in understanding.

Abstract:

> The injury created functional limitations.

Concrete:

> The hand that carried the spear now hurt when it closed around wood.

## 10.1 Narration-image relationship

Use three relationships:

1. **Reinforcement** — image makes the narration instantly clear.
2. **Expansion** — image adds meaning narration does not need to say.
3. **Counterpoint** — image creates irony, tension, humor, or contradiction.

Avoid permanent reinforcement. If narration and image always say the same thing, the video feels illustrated rather than directed.

## 10.2 Write for the ear, not only the page

When narration will be spoken by a human or synthesized by TTS, optimize for listening comprehension and prosody. A sentence that looks elegant on the page may be difficult to hear if it contains too many nested clauses, numbers, qualifications, or abstract nouns.

Prefer:

```text
concrete state
→ clear development
→ short turn
→ explanation / consequence
→ payoff or forward pressure
```

Do not make every sentence equally short or equally long. Controlled variation creates rhythm.

A useful spoken pattern is:

```text
medium
→ medium
→ short turn
→ medium-long explanation
→ short or medium payoff
```

A short sentence is emphasis. It is not the default unit of narration.

## 10.3 Spoken blocks and cross-sentence context

Think in **spoken blocks**, not isolated clever sentences. A spoken block is a small group of related sentences that forms one coherent thought and gives the narrator enough semantic context to shape pacing and emphasis.

Usually prefer roughly 2–5 related sentences per block when the material supports it. This is not a quota.

Good spoken blocks often contain some combination of:

- setup;
- development;
- one short punch or reversal;
- consequence;
- transition into the next question.

Do not create a new paragraph after every short sentence merely to look cinematic. Several tiny standalone fragments can make synthetic narration sound clipped, reset intonation repeatedly, or lose cross-sentence momentum.

If deliberate repetition is useful, keep the repeated lines in the same semantic block whenever possible:

```text
Someone has to notice the hunger. Someone has to stop working. Someone has to prepare the food.

And then somebody has to hold the child, feed slowly, and be ready to do it again.
```

The repetition creates rhythm; the final expansion breaks the pattern.

## 10.4 Punctuation is prosody guidance

Use punctuation to mark real semantic and prosodic boundaries. Do not decorate the Script with punctuation merely to force drama.

Treat punctuation approximately as:

```text
,    micro-pause / phrase grouping
.    normal thought boundary
—    fast pivot, interruption, contrast, or reveal within one thought
:    setup followed by explanation, list, reveal, or question
?    genuine question / open-loop contour
paragraph break    major thought, scene, evidence-mode, or emotional boundary
```

Use semicolons sparingly in spoken entertainment narration; two clear sentences are often easier to hear.

Use exclamation marks rarely. Excitement should come from wording, stakes, contrast, and delivery—not punctuation inflation.

Use ellipses rarely. Prefer a clear comma, em dash, period, or sentence structure unless hesitation itself is meaningful.

Do not rely on repeated `!`, `...`, ALL CAPS, or stage-direction brackets as a substitute for strong prosody.

## 10.5 Sentence arrangement for TTS

Make syntax easy for a speech model to parse.

Prefer one dominant syntactic path per sentence. When a sentence contains several clauses, numbers, proper nouns, dates, evidence qualifications, or chronological stages, serialize the information into multiple sentences.

Weak for speech:

> In one child, researchers found seven months of exclusive breastfeeding, followed by seven months of supplementation, then an abrupt end at 1.2 years.

Stronger for speech:

> One child preserved an unusually detailed feeding record. For about seven months, the child appears to have been exclusively breastfed. Then other foods entered the diet. Around 1.2 years, breastfeeding seems to have stopped abruptly.

Keep turn words near the front of the sentence when they signal an important change:

- but;
- then;
- so;
- instead;
- and yet;
- now;
- except;
- until.

A clear turn is often stronger than hiding the contrast inside a subordinate clause.

## 10.6 Evidence must become spoken discovery

For evidence-heavy sections, prefer:

```text
concrete clue / surprising result
→ what it means
→ limitation
→ story consequence
```

over:

```text
abstract concept
→ methodology lecture
→ statistic
→ conclusion
```

Introduce technical terms only after the viewer understands why the evidence matters.

When numbers matter, separate them into a sequence the ear can follow. Do not pack several ages, percentages, dates, and caveats into one long sentence.

Evidence sections must still create movement. Every factual paragraph should answer an active question, break an assumption, narrow uncertainty, or force the story toward the next test.

## 10.7 Narration pacing and planned speaking rate

Do not assume one universal narration speed fits every topic. When the active task permits pacing choice, select the planned average speaking pace from the actual storytelling mode, audience, terminology load, information density, emotional rhythm, and intended energy.

Treat the planned average pace as a whole-video planning value, not a command that every section or sentence must be delivered at identical speed.

Dense evidence, unfamiliar terminology, and reflective payoff may need more breathing room. Concrete action, escalation, lists, and comic reversals may tolerate faster local delivery.

If the task supplies an explicit runtime target, write for that runtime under the active contract rather than padding or compressing every video toward a universal word count.

## 10.8 Final spoken-TTS pass

After story, evidence, retention, and visuality are already strong—but **before Script freeze**—run a final spoken pass. This pass is not a new story rewrite. It protects meaning while optimizing oral delivery.

For every section:

1. group related short sentences into coherent spoken blocks;
2. split sentences that overload the listener with clauses, numbers, or qualifications;
3. vary sentence length intentionally;
4. place commas, periods, em dashes, colons, and questions at meaningful boundaries;
5. reserve paragraph breaks for major thought or story changes;
6. remove accidental staccato caused by too many isolated fragments;
7. remove accidental monotony caused by repeated sentence length and structure;
8. preserve exact factual meaning and evidence confidence;
9. keep title-promised identity and canonical character semantics unchanged;
10. confirm the climax answer lands before its qualifications bury it.

A good spoken-TTS pass should make the Script easier to perform **without adding theatrical clutter**.

Do not insert SSML, pronunciation markup, pause tags, phonemes, or engine-specific directives unless the active contract explicitly requests them.

# 11. Evidence-led historical storytelling

Historical entertainment must remain cinematic without pretending reconstruction is documentation.

## 11.1 Separate three levels

### Documented
Directly supported by evidence.

### Inferred
Reasonable interpretation supported by evidence.

### Reconstructed
A plausible scenario created to make constraints experientially understandable.

Do not silently promote:

```text
reconstructed → inferred
inferred → documented
```

## 11.2 Reconstruction boundary

When following an imagined protagonist:

- preserve the same protagonist for story continuity;
- do not imply the protagonist is a named archaeological individual unless supported;
- do not invent exact treatment, motive, dialogue, or relationship as historical fact;
- use comparative evidence to constrain plausibility;
- visually distinguish comparative evidence from the protagonist's reconstruction.

## 11.3 Evidence should change the story

Do not include research only because it is interesting.

Ask:

> What problem does this evidence solve for the viewer right now?

If it does not change understanding, remove it or move it.

## 11.4 Research discipline when web search is available

When the authoring environment provides web search, use it when verification materially improves accuracy, especially for:

- current or time-sensitive claims;
- niche or uncertain claims;
- precise historical, archaeological, scientific, medical, legal, or numerical statements;
- disputed interpretations;
- unfamiliar terms or entities;
- visual details whose accuracy affects reconstruction.

Prefer primary research, official documentation, museums, archives, government or institutional sources, and other authoritative evidence when available.

Research should resolve an active authoring uncertainty. Do not browse merely to decorate the Script with extra facts.

For Script authoring, finish material factual research **before Script freeze**. After freeze, do not silently rewrite narration because of later browsing; return to pre-freeze authoring if the evidence requires prose changes.

For Scene Breakdown, research may improve **how supported content is depicted**. It must not change what the frozen Script claims, invent a new canonical identity, or promote reconstruction into documentation.

# 12. Canonical visual identity semantics

This section is critical for high-quality image-driven Scene Breakdown.

A visual reference is an **identity/continuity lock**, not a list of everything visible in the frame.

## 12.1 Canonical individual

A canonical individual reference means:

> This exact recurring person's identity must remain consistent.

Use when the same person matters across images.

Do not create a new canonical identity merely because the script says:

- a companion;
- another hunter;
- one worker;
- a nearby observer;
- somebody else.

An incidental unnamed person may appear visually without becoming a recurring identity.

## 12.2 Canonical group

A group reference means:

> This recurring group identity matters as a group.

It does **not** mean “one helper from that group.”

If a group reference is selected:

- the visual should genuinely require multiple members or group-level continuity;
- framing should allow the intended group to read clearly;
- downstream visual direction should preserve plurality;
- do not collapse the group into a solo portrait;
- do not use the group reference merely because one unnamed member appears.

Correct:

```text
group prepares equipment
group crosses terrain
group workflow redistributes labor
hunting formation changes direction
```

Usually do not bind group identity for:

```text
one companion reaches for spear
another hunter looks at his hand
one helper receives an object
one observer notices a mistake
```

## 12.3 Never invent canonical IDs downstream

If an identity does not exist in the upstream canonical catalog, do not invent a new recurring reference during Scene Breakdown merely to make a visual convenient.

Either:

- use an incidental unnamed person with no identity lock; or
- if the story truly requires a recurring new character, fix it at the upstream character-design layer.

Do not repair downstream semantics by fabricating upstream continuity.

# 13. Other reference semantics

## 13.1 Prop reference

A prop reference is for a recurring recognizable object whose continuity matters.

Bind it when:

- the object is visibly important;
- the same object carries story meaning across beats;
- later callbacks depend on recognition;
- object identity affects the generated image.

Do not bind merely because the narration mentions the noun.

## 13.2 Location reference

A location reference is a canonical environment identity anchor.

Use selectively when:

- geography matters;
- the location must be recognized;
- an establishing shot needs the environment;
- a callback depends on the same place;
- spatial continuity would otherwise be lost.

Do **not** bind the location to every close-up simply because the beat occurs there.

Remember:

```text
beat occurs at location
≠
beat requires location identity lock
```

Use environment description for contextual world continuity when exact location identity is unnecessary.

## 13.3 Recurring visual subject

A recurring visual subject is a specific repeated visual idea or body/object detail.

Bind only when the current beat actually depicts that subject.

Check semantic direction carefully.

Example:

```text
injured right hand
≠
left-hand adaptation
```

A left-hand training beat must not accidentally bind the injured-right-hand subject simply because both are part of the same recovery story.

### 13.3.1 Physical specimen vs analytical derivative

A recurring physical specimen and an analytical visualization derived from that specimen do not automatically share the same reference authority.

Examples:

```text
physical fossil tooth
≠
chemical concentration map inside that tooth

skeletal specimen
≠
CT slice / density map

ice core
≠
isotope profile derived from the core

artifact
≠
microscopic residue or scan derived from the artifact
```

Use the physical object as a recurring prop when recognizable object identity matters.

Use a recurring visual subject for the analytical derivative when its visual semantics differ materially from the physical object's appearance.

Do not make a prop reference implicitly authorize every derived map, scan, overlay, cross-section, reconstruction, or analytical graphic.

### 13.3.2 Prefer evidence form over explanatory metaphor

When scientific or historical evidence has a concrete physical or measurable form, canonicalize the **form in which the evidence actually exists**, not the metaphor used to explain it.

Prefer:

```text
spatial barium distribution across growth-aligned tooth tissue
```

over:

```text
feeding timeline
chemical line
nursing graph
```

Prefer:

```text
oxygen-isotope variation across growth layers
```

over:

```text
climate timeline
```

Prefer:

```text
enamel defect / growth interruption
```

over:

```text
stress line
```

Narration may use metaphors such as timeline, clock, fingerprint, diary, or signal because they help viewers understand the evidence. Those metaphors do not automatically become canonical visual-subject identity.

The recurring subject should preserve the physical or analytical evidence form first. Scene-level visuals may translate it into simplified explanatory compositions only when the evidence remains recognizable and the translation does not create unsupported semantics.

## 13.4 Motif

A motif is recurring **meaning**, not identity.

Examples:

- grip as capability;
- help resisted, then accepted;
- frame widening individual → group;
- drop → reaction → retry → hold.

Use motifs when the repeated pattern is narratively active.

Do not force motif refs into every adjacent beat.

# 14. Scene Breakdown: beat segmentation

Scene Breakdown is direction, not sentence illustration.

## 14.1 Beat test

Create a new visual beat when there is a meaningful change in one or more of:

- dominant action;
- decisive consequence;
- subject;
- location;
- time;
- power relationship;
- emotional state;
- scale;
- evidence mode;
- visual motif function;
- question being answered.

Do not split only because punctuation changed.

Do not merge only to reduce beat count.

## 14.2 One still = one dominant time-state

For still-image generation, each cinematic beat should normally depict **one decisive instant**.

Bad:

> He reaches for the spear, slips, hands it over, looks ashamed, and walks away.

That contains several chronological states.

Choose:

> His foot slips while the spear is still in his hand and the companion's hand waits inches away.

Then the next beat can show the handoff.

Exception:

A deliberate explanatory graphic/layout may compare multiple states when the visual grammar explicitly calls for sequential comparison.

## 14.3 Beat sufficiency question

Ask:

> Can one image communicate the intended story change clearly without requiring the viewer to imagine several sequential actions inside the same frame?

If no, split or redesign.

## 14.4 Avoid over-segmentation

Do not create separate beats for fragments that produce the same visual state.

If two narration lines resolve into one decisive image, keep one beat.

Beat count is an outcome of story state changes, not a target.

## 14.5 Pacing density semantics

`high / medium / low` describe the frequency of **genuine independently drawable visual/story-state changes** in narration.

They do not describe importance, sentence density, rhetorical intensity, section title, or desired image count.

### High

`high` means the narration naturally supports **many distinct visual beats** without Scene Breakdown having to fragment punctuation or invent visual material.

Typical `high` progression may include frequent changes in:

- visible subject;
- action / reaction;
- evidence state;
- process state;
- comparison side;
- cause / consequence;
- attempt / failure;
- contradiction;
- scale;
- question;
- reveal;
- visual gag;
- interpretation that materially changes what should be shown.

A section is not `high` merely because it contains many short lines such as:

```text
This matters.
The evidence is strange.
The answer changes.
The mystery deepens.
```

If those lines still resolve to the same dominant visual state, actual visual density is lower.

### Medium

`medium` means **regular visual progression with deliberate absorption space**.

A coherent visual may support several related sentences while the narration explains its meaning, limitation, or consequence. New visual states still arrive regularly, but less frequently than in `high`.

Typical rhythm:

```text
visual state A
→ explain / absorb A
→ visual state B
→ qualify B
→ consequence C
```

### Low

`low` means **fewer but stronger visual states designed to hold**.

Low density is appropriate for reflection, evidence inspection, tension, emotional aftermath, a restrained climax, or resolution when one image gains meaning as narration develops.

`low` must not be used to excuse abstract or visually empty prose. A long hold should be intentional because the visual state is strong, not because the Script failed to create visual material.

### Stable density semantics vs selected video pacing

The semantic meaning of section `high / medium / low` is stable across videos. It describes how frequently the narration itself creates genuine independently drawable visual/story-state changes.

The video-level pacing mode is a different control. The active task may select `fast`, `medium`, or `slow` and supply the exact deterministic `video_pacing_profile` for that mode. Each section density then has one `preferred_hold_seconds` band that expresses the intended visual-refresh feel for this video.

Do not confuse:

```text
video pacing mode: fast / medium / slow
section visual density: high / medium / low
```

Use only the profile supplied by the active task. Do not invent, retune, or substitute numeric ranges.

The preferred band is not:

- a per-beat hard minimum or maximum;
- a beat quota;
- an image quota;
- a word envelope;
- a rule to cut narration every N seconds;
- permission to manufacture a visual/story state.

During Script authoring it is a **binding authoring target for realized section-level semantic-state cadence**. During Scene Breakdown it is a **binding reason to perform pacing-conformance semantic rescan after boundaries exist**. In neither stage may arithmetic override semantic truth.

### Section-level narration pacing

Narration pace and visual pace are related creative controls but are not the same variable.

Before drafting prose, choose and lock a `planned_narration_wpm` for every Script section within the active task's defensive range. Base it on that section's narrative function, terminology load, information density, emotional function, and intended spoken performance. A hook may be delivered faster than a reflective ending; a technical mechanism may require a different spoken pace from a climax even when both have `high` visual density.

Do not assume one WPM applies uniformly across the entire video. Do not change a section's WPM after drafting merely to disguise a runtime miss. If total planned runtime is wrong, revise section development, compression, evidence/story progression, or allocation.

When estimating realized visual cadence before freeze or estimating beat duration after semantic segmentation, use the owning section's locked narration WPM. Whole-video average WPM, when exposed by the active contract, is an aggregate diagnostic rather than the authoring speed of every section. Actual TTS speech, pauses, and prosody remain the final timing authority downstream.

### 14.5.1 Planned density during Script authoring

Before writing full prose, privately assign a **planned visual density** to every major section.

For standard image-driven long-form entertainment, use role as a creative starting point, not a blind mapping:

- hook / cold open → normally `high`;
- visually active mechanism, reconstruction, comparison, escalation, or reversal → often `high`;
- setup, explanation, false solution, clarification → often `medium`;
- crisis → `medium` or `low` depending whether the story needs active testing or deliberate hold;
- climax → `high`, `medium`, or occasionally `low` according to the decisive test, but evidence-heavy multi-step climaxes should usually be planned `high`;
- resolution / reflective callback → usually `low`.

If the active task or channel format specifies a different strategy, follow that explicit strategy.

Planned density is a **writing requirement**.

- Planned `high` → the prose itself must generate many genuine drawable visual/story states.
- Planned `medium` → the prose must generate regular visual progression with meaningful absorption space.
- Planned `low` → the prose must generate fewer, stronger states that can hold without becoming visually empty.

Scene Breakdown must **reveal** this density, not manufacture it.

### 14.5.2 Section visual-progression plan

For each planned section, privately define before drafting prose:

1. `section_role`;
2. `narrative_job`;
3. `planned_density`;
4. `visual_progression` — the ordered concrete subjects, evidence states, actions, comparisons, transformations, consequences, or callbacks the narration should naturally make drawable;
5. `section_exit_state` — the final image/state/question that hands the viewer into the next section.

Example:

```text
section_role: cold_open
planned_density: high

visual_progression:
anonymous child
→ surviving tooth
→ tooth growth layers
→ mapped feeding evidence
→ mixed feeding
→ abrupt disruption
→ competing possible causes
→ later survival
→ unresolved timestamp

section_exit_state:
the event is precisely timed, but its cause remains unknown
```

The plan is internal authoring scaffolding. Do not expose it unless the active contract requests it.

### 14.5.3 Pre-freeze visual-generativity gate

After drafting each section but **before Script freeze**, map the finished spoken blocks back to potential dominant visual states.

Ask:

> If Scene Breakdown may only use what this narration genuinely supplies, does the section naturally support the planned density?

#### Planned high passes only when:

- many independently drawable visual/story states are genuinely present;
- state changes are distributed across the section rather than concentrated in one short burst;
- the section does not rely on rhetorical fragments to simulate visual change;
- several consecutive spoken blocks do not collapse to the same tooth, face, graph, blank field, object, environment, or abstract layout;
- a downstream Scene Breakdown could create frequent meaningful beats without inventing actions, evidence, characters, or unsupported comparisons.

#### Planned medium passes only when:

- visual states arrive regularly;
- explanatory holds are coherent and intentional;
- the section does not remain on one visual idea for a long abstract stretch without story reason.

#### Planned low passes only when:

- the smaller number of visual states is intentional;
- each major state is strong enough to hold;
- meaning deepens, reverses, or resolves while the image holds;
- low density is not caused by generic or visually empty prose.

If the section fails, rewrite the **Script**, not the density label.

Do not add fake visual variety such as arbitrary researcher gestures, unrelated cutaways, decorative locations, or invented human behavior. Increase visual generativity through story, evidence, physical states, comparisons, consequences, contradictions, or supported reconstruction.

### 14.5.4 Selected video pacing profile

When the active task supplies `user_input.pacing_mode` and its resolved `video_pacing_profile`, use the profile exactly as supplied. The profile is selected per video, not inferred again inside Script or Scene Breakdown.

Each section density supplies:

```text
preferred_hold_seconds
```

It answers:

> Roughly how quickly should meaningful visual/story states refresh in this selected video mode when the section genuinely has this density?

It does not answer:

> How many beats must this section contain?

During Script authoring, combine the planned section density, locked section `planned_narration_wpm`, and the matching preferred band. After drafting, inventory genuine drawable states, collapse same-dominant-still states, estimate realized cadence, and treat a material mismatch as a failed authoring check when supported progression is still available. Rewrite the Script before freeze rather than asking Scene Breakdown to invent states downstream.

Do not divide section duration by the range and manufacture a target number of events.

During Scene Breakdown, compare resulting semantic holds to the preferred range only after first-pass segmentation and rhetorical merge-back are stable. Repeated long holds require an active rescan for missed genuine states; repeated short holds require an active rescan for false fragmentation. Change segmentation only when meaning supports the change.

### 14.5.5 Independent pacing-density classification in Script Analysis

After the Script is legitimately frozen, Script Analysis must classify the **actual frozen narration**, independent of the author's earlier planned density.

For each section:

1. identify the genuine independently drawable visual/story-state changes in the frozen narration;
2. ignore sentence count, paragraph count, rhetorical emphasis, and role labels;
3. compare the actual change pattern with `high / medium / low`;
4. assign the density that the frozen text truly supports.

Do not preserve a planned `high` label when the frozen Script only supports `medium`.

If a mismatch is discovered **before freeze**, return to Script authoring and rewrite when the intended density is important to the video architecture.

If a mismatch is discovered only after legitimate freeze, Analysis must report the actual density and must not silently rewrite the Script.

### 14.5.6 Pacing-profile conformance in Scene Breakdown

After semantic beat boundaries have been drafted and semantically reconciled, estimate beat duration from exact beat words and the owning section's locked `planned_narration_wpm`, then compare the section rhythm with the selected video's matching `preferred_hold_seconds` band.

A material mismatch is a **mandatory semantic-review trigger**, never permission to split or merge mechanically.

- if many semantic beats hold materially above the preferred range, actively rescan for missed action/reaction, evidence-state, comparison, consequence, reversal, question, scale, interpretation, or visual-payoff changes;
- if many beats hold materially below the preferred range, actively rescan for rhetorical fragments, syntactic fragmentation, repeated evidence states, and adjacent beats whose strongest still would be materially the same;
- if the rescan finds a genuine missed state, split because that state exists;
- if the rescan finds false fragmentation, merge because one stable state exists;
- if no semantic correction exists, preserve the truthful beat even when it remains outside the preferred band.

Correct:

```text
pacing feels off
→ rescan the exact narration
→ find a genuine missed or unnecessary visual/story state
→ change segmentation only because meaning justifies it
```

Incorrect:

```text
section duration ÷ preferred seconds
→ required beat count
→ manufacture cuts until the number matches
```

It is valid for individual beats to differ substantially from the preferred value when the story state genuinely calls for it.

# 15. Visual concept

`visual_concept` should capture the **single memorable visual idea** of the beat.

Ask:

> If the viewer remembered only one image idea from this beat, what would it be?

Good:

> The wrist becomes the hinge between falling body weight and stone.

Good:

> The spear sits between the hunter's reaching hand and another hunter's gaze, turning readiness into an unspoken question.

Weak:

> Show the story clearly.

Weak:

> The image is organized around the beat's decisive change.

Weak:

> Use cinematic composition to communicate tension.

Those are authoring instructions, not beat-specific concepts.

## 15.1 Semantic uniqueness, not synonym uniqueness

Do not mechanically force every visual concept to use different vocabulary.

Intentional callbacks may repeat the same underlying concept.

But repeated concepts must evolve:

```text
introduction → failure → adaptation → pressure test → payoff
```

Do not hide repetition by paraphrasing the same generic concept 20 times.

# 16. Subject action

`subject_action` should state what is visibly happening in the selected instant.

It should be:

- concrete;
- drawable;
- temporally coherent;
- emotionally legible through behavior;
- compatible with the chosen references.

Avoid backend language.

Avoid authoring commentary.

Avoid abstract conclusions that cannot be photographed.

Prefer:

> His right fingers begin to open around the spear, then stop before contact is lost.

Over:

> The visual demonstrates partial functional recovery.

# 17. Composition and blocking

Composition should explain **spatial meaning**.

It should answer relevant questions such as:

- where is the focal subject?
- where is the important object?
- what occupies foreground / midground / background?
- who watches whom?
- who has physical access to the object?
- what space separates characters?
- what line or shape guides the eye?
- where is negative space?
- what relationship does the staging reveal?

Do not duplicate `subject_action`.

Example:

```text
Subject action:
The companion reaches for the spear while the hunter keeps hold.

Composition:
Form a triangle between the hunter, the waiting hand, and the spear, leaving negative space around the shaft so the choice reads instantly.
```

Blocking should arise from motivation and relationship, not decorative symmetry.

# 18. Camera grammar

Keep these concepts separate:

```text
framing
= how much of the subject/world is included

camera angle
= camera's perspective relative to the subject

composition
= where subjects/objects sit inside the frame

camera movement
= how viewpoint changes during the shot

orientation
= profile / frontal / three-quarter / rear / over-the-shoulder staging
```

Do not confuse them.

## 18.1 Camera angle choices

Use the active schema's allowed vocabulary.

Conceptually:

- **eye level** — neutral human observation;
- **low angle** — upward view, pressure, physical dominance, ground emphasis;
- **high angle** — downward view, vulnerability, spatial relation;
- **top-down / overhead** — process, geometry, object relationships, detachment;
- **Dutch / canted** — visible instability or disorientation; use rarely;
- **POV** — subjective perception itself matters;
- **neutral graphic** — diagrams, evidence plates, anatomical comparisons.

## 18.2 OTS and profile

Over-the-shoulder and profile are usually **framing/orientation/composition ideas**, not automatically camera-angle values.

Example:

```text
camera angle: eye level
composition: frame over the companion's shoulder toward the hunter
```

Do not expand a camera-angle enum merely because composition needs more descriptive language.

## 18.3 No angle quota

Do not force variety statistically.

Use an unusual angle only when it adds meaning.

Before finalizing, however, verify that the output has not defaulted to one angle simply from habit when overhead, POV, low/high, or another allowed angle would communicate the beat more clearly.

---

# 19. Shot scale

Choose scale by story function.

- **Extreme wide** — world, journey, isolation, geography.
- **Wide** — physical relationships, group dynamics, environment.
- **Medium** — action, body language, two-person interaction.
- **Close-up** — decision, reaction, evidence, hand/object detail.
- **Extreme close-up** — precise clue, failure point, tactile payoff.
- **Graphic layout** — comparative evidence or deliberate multi-state explanation.

Do not use close-ups continuously.

Do not select a group identity and then frame so tightly that the group cannot meaningfully exist.

---

# 20. Visual causality

Whenever possible, make the image show **cause and consequence in one spatial relationship**.

Examples:

- injured hand guarded while the opposite arm carries the load;
- overloaded hand trapped between gear and the need for balance;
- spear between offered help and pride;
- dropped blade between the protagonist and the person who noticed;
- protagonist in one depth plane while redistributed work happens in another.

Prefer active relationships over portraits.

---

# 21. Visual motifs and callbacks

Use a small number of motifs.

A strong motif evolves:

```text
introduction
→ variation
→ failure
→ adaptation
→ pressure test
→ payoff
```

Example:

```text
spear grip works normally
→ grip fails
→ spear must be surrendered
→ grip becomes practice test
→ grip returns under pressure
→ final callback shows limited recovery
```

A callback should not merely repeat the first image.

It should reverse or deepen its meaning.

# 22. Narration delivery identity

Narration style is **channel-owned creative intent**.

Read the active Channel DNA before deciding how the narrator should sound. Do not assume that high-retention YouTube narration must be funny, playful, conversational, dramatic, solemn, soothing, documentary-like, sarcastic, poetic, frightening, or high-energy.

The same topic may legitimately be authored as:

- an evidence-led entertaining explainer;
- a serious documentary;
- an atmospheric or midnight storyteller piece;
- a casefile / tension-led investigation;
- a warm children's explainer;
- a philosophical minimalist essay;
- a horror narrator;
- a hybrid or a completely novel channel voice.

Visual style does not determine narration style. Pacing mode does not determine narration style. Topic niche does not determine narration style. Use the supplied Channel DNA narration identity as authority.

## 22.1 Narration identity dimensions

When Channel DNA supplies structured narration guidance, interpret the fields together:

- **voice** — narrator personality, stance, linguistic character, distance from the viewer, and degree of narrator presence;
- **delivery** — overall editorial feel, energy, restraint, rhythm, seriousness, warmth, intensity, or other channel-specific balance;
- **explanation style** — how difficult information becomes understandable, memorable, sensory, concrete, or emotionally legible;
- **tone boundaries** — creative, factual, ethical, or audience-fit lines the voice must not cross;
- **avoid** — voice drift, habits, devices, tonal modes, or stylistic failures that should not appear.

Treat these as semantic creative instructions, not keywords to parrot back into narration.

## 22.2 Open creative architecture

Do not force the Channel DNA into a closed profile taxonomy.

A channel may combine attributes that normally belong to different styles, for example:

```text
measured documentary authority
+
very simple explanatory language
+
occasional dry narrator reaction
+
melancholic endings
```

That hybrid is valid when the Channel DNA requests it.

A narration identity may also describe a style that is not represented by any reference example in this Skill. Follow the supplied identity directly rather than choosing the nearest named archetype.

## 22.3 Illustrative reference calibrations

The following are **teaching examples only**. They are not product modes, schema enums, required ratios, or an exhaustive set of styles.

### Reference A — Evidence-led entertaining explainer

A possible overall editorial feel:

```text
~65–70% factual / evidence / story substance
~30–35% entertaining explanation and delivery
```

The second component does **not** mean jokes. It may come from:

- concrete translation of technical facts;
- human-scale problem framing;
- relatable contrasts;
- concise narrator reactions;
- physical or logistical consequences that are naturally amusing;
- memorable simplification;
- lightly playful wording when appropriate;
- visual or verbal irony already supported by the real situation.

Core rule:

> Make the real fact fun, vivid, or easy to understand. Never invent a funnier fact.

For historical/scientific work, do not fabricate dialogue, motives, caregivers, meals, reactions, artifacts, actions, or events merely to make the explanation more entertaining.

### Reference B — Serious documentary

A possible overall editorial feel:

```text
~85–90% factual / story / evidence substance
~10–15% expressive framing
```

Typical traits may include:

- measured authority;
- precise transitions;
- restrained narrator personality;
- limited analogy;
- minimal levity;
- careful confidence language.

Do not make it casual merely because another channel profile would.

### Reference C — Atmospheric / midnight storyteller

A possible overall editorial feel:

```text
~60–70% story progression / information
~30–40% atmosphere, sensory immersion, rhythm, and emotional space
```

Typical traits may include:

- calm or low-stimulation voice;
- gentle transitions;
- sensory description;
- fewer punchy reversals;
- deliberate repetition when it deepens atmosphere;
- little or no playful framing when playfulness would break immersion.

Do not force entertaining-explainer devices into a channel whose purpose is soothing or contemplative narration.

### Reference D — Casefile / tension-led factual narration

A possible overall editorial feel:

```text
~80–90% factual / case / evidence progression
~10–20% controlled tension and narrative framing
```

Typical traits may include:

- precise chronology or evidence progression;
- restrained tension;
- careful uncertainty;
- minimal or zero humor around harm;
- suspense created by what the record reveals, not by fabricated withholding or sensationalism.

Again, these ratios describe **overall editorial feel only**. Never convert them into word counts, sentence quotas, joke frequency, mandatory analogies, or validators.

## 22.4 Per-section delivery continuity

A channel voice must survive the entire Script.

A common failure is:

```text
hook
→ vivid, channel-specific voice

middle evidence
→ generic explainer prose

uncertainty / climax
→ academic-summary voice
```

Do not allow evidence complexity to erase the narrator identity.

For every section, privately ask:

- Does this still sound like the same channel?
- Did the narrator disappear or become more theatrical than the Channel DNA permits?
- Did technical material become needlessly academic?
- Did serious material become inappropriately playful?
- Did atmospheric material become too punchy or stimulating?
- Did tension become sensational rather than evidence-led?
- Did a stylistic device violate `tone_boundaries` or `avoid`?

Rewrite drift before freeze.

Scientific or historical caution should change **claim confidence**, not automatically change the selected voice.

## 22.5 Entertaining explanation when appropriate

When the active Channel DNA supports accessible, vivid, or entertaining explanation, prefer this pattern:

```text
real fact
→ concrete consequence
→ intuitive or human-scale framing
→ concise reaction / contrast when useful
→ next evidence state
```

Avoid:

```text
technical fact
→ detached joke
→ return to lecture
```

The goal is not comedy density. The goal is that explanation itself is enjoyable to follow.

Good entertainment sources include:

- a real practical inconvenience;
- a real contradiction;
- a real physical consequence;
- a real tradeoff;
- a verified surprising comparison;
- an evidence-supported absurdity;
- a narrator reaction to what the evidence genuinely does or does not allow.

## 22.6 Humor when the Channel DNA supports it

Humor may emerge from:

- overconfidence;
- failed competence;
- misunderstanding;
- delayed reaction;
- dignity under obvious failure;
- serious behavior in an absurd practical situation;
- callbacks;
- another character noticing what the protagonist hopes went unseen.

For a recurring gag when the channel actually uses gags:

```text
setup → escalation → social consequence → payoff
```

Do not make pain itself the joke.

Do not joke through every serious beat.

Silence and restraint often make visual comedy stronger.

When the Channel DNA does **not** support humor, do not introduce it merely because the Skill contains this subsection.

## 22.7 Style never overrides evidence

Narration style is not factual authority.

A playful analogy, poetic image, ominous phrase, deadpan line, or visual metaphor must not become a new historical/scientific claim.

For example, a line such as:

> the chewing department had mostly shut down

may support a symbolic visual metaphor in a compatible channel style. It does not document a literal department, worker, reaction, or event.

Keep these layers separate:

```text
evidence
→ what is supported

narration delivery
→ how the supported idea is communicated

visual metaphor
→ how that communication may be represented symbolically
```

Never allow the latter two layers to silently rewrite the first.

# 23. Topic Idea workflow

A strong Topic Idea is a complete long-form story opportunity.

Before accepting an idea, check:

- target viewer is clear;
- central question is immediately understandable;
- title promise can be fulfilled;
- conflict exists;
- evidence path exists;
- visual anchor exists;
- middle can escalate;
- there is at least one reinterpretation;
- ending can pay off the opening;
- humor can arise naturally if appropriate;
- research and visuals are realistically producible.

Reject ideas fully answerable in one paragraph.

## 23.1 Character lens

Broad:

> How prehistoric people survived injury.

Stronger:

> Could a Stone Age hunter survive after breaking the hand he used for everything?

A character-scale question provides:

- body;
- practical stakes;
- continuity;
- attempts;
- relationships;
- visual motifs;
- a testable ending.

# 24. Script workflow

Before writing full prose:

## Step 1 — One-sentence movie
Define protagonist, problem, escalation, payoff.

## Step 2 — Central promise
State exactly what the title/thumbnail promise the viewer.

## Step 3 — Question ladder
List how curiosity evolves.

## Step 4 — Section architecture and visual-density plan

Plan the complete section sequence before prose.

For every planned section privately define:

- `section_role`;
- `narrative_job`;
- `planned_density`;
- `visual_progression`;
- `section_exit_state`.

For standard long-form image-driven entertainment when compatible with the active Channel DNA narration identity:

- the opening hook / cold open may normally be `high`;
- do not let several adjacent major sections all default to the same density without a story reason;
- use density changes to create controlled rhythm across the full video;
- plan high-density sections around genuine changes in evidence, action, consequence, comparison, contradiction, scale, question, or interpretation;
- plan low-density sections around strong images that can deepen while narration holds.

Use the exact selected per-video profile supplied by the active task. Combine it with each section's planned density and locked narration WPM during authoring review, but never calculate a required beat count.

## Step 5 — Causal handoff plan
For every planned major section after setup, state privately what unresolved consequence, failed solution, sharper question, reversal, or new need from the previous section makes this section necessary now. If the handoff is only topical adjacency, redesign the progression.

## Step 6 — Evidence spine
For factual videos, map each important claim to its narrative purpose. Evidence should solve an active story problem, alter an interpretation, create a consequence, or narrow uncertainty.

## Step 7 — Character pressure
For each section where character pressure matters define:
- current goal;
- obstacle;
- attempt;
- consequence;
- emotional change.

Do not invent a character-pressure structure for evidence-only passages that genuinely do not need one.

## Step 8 — Visual anchors
Choose:
- opening image;
- first major disruption or failure;
- midpoint image;
- crisis image;
- climax image;
- final callback.

These anchors do not replace the section-level `visual_progression`; they are the strongest memorable states in the full-video arc.

## Step 9 — Draft for momentum and visual generativity

Write progression before polishing sentences.

Write each section so its **actual narration supports its planned density**.

For `high` sections, create frequent concrete changes in what can be shown. Prefer sequences such as:

```text
clue
→ changed clue
→ comparison
→ contradiction
→ consequence
→ reveal
```

Do not simulate high density through staccato prose.

For `medium`, allow coherent explanation around each visual state while introducing new states regularly.

For `low`, deliberately hold fewer strong states and deepen their meaning.

## Step 10 — Rewrite for spoken voice
Improve rhythm, compression, subtext, concrete language, and fidelity to the active Channel DNA narration identity. Preserve humor only when the supplied channel intent supports it; preserve restraint, calmness, formality, tension, lyricism, minimalism, or another delivery when that is the supplied intent. Prefer spoken language that sounds native to the channel over generic polished essay prose. Replace abstract thesis statements with concrete action, evidence, comparison, or consequence when possible.

Do not destroy a good visual progression merely to make prose sound more literary.

## Step 11 — Rewrite for evidence integration
Move explanation next to the problem it explains. Convert evidence into:

```text
concrete clue
→ meaning
→ limitation
→ story consequence
```

whenever appropriate.

## Step 12 — Visual-generativity and density-match pass

Run §14.5.3 on every section.

Privately map spoken blocks to potential dominant visuals.

If:

```text
planned high
→ actual narration repeatedly resolves to the same visual state
```

rewrite the Script before freeze.

If a planned medium or low section does not match its intended function, either rewrite it or deliberately revise the **plan before freeze**. Do not merely rename a failed high section as medium when the story architecture actually needs high visual density there.

Verify especially:

- opening hook;
- major evidence mechanism;
- midpoint reversal;
- comparative escalation;
- climax.

These are common places where long-form image-driven videos need strong visual progression.

## Step 13 — Rewrite for visuality
Ensure each paragraph or spoken block suggests concrete actions, relationships, objects, evidence states, transformations, comparisons, or spatial consequences when the story supports them.

Remove production-direction language from viewer-facing narration unless intentionally part of the voice.

Avoid abstract stretches in which several consecutive blocks would all require the same generic infographic or evidence plate.

## Step 14 — Final spoken-TTS pass
Run §10.8 across the complete Script. Recheck spoken blocks, sentence-length variation, punctuation, evidence-number phrasing, paragraph boundaries, and climax delivery. Preserve meaning while improving oral performance.

## Step 15 — Story architecture gate
Before a Script can be considered complete, privately verify all of the following:

- **Central question** — one exact dramatic question survives from opening to payoff.
- **Question ladder** — each major answer creates a sharper complication, interpretation, or question.
- **Causal handoffs** — every major section after setup is made necessary by what came before.
- **Density architecture** — the sequence of planned densities creates intentional visual rhythm rather than accidental sameness.
- **Opening density** — the hook/cold open genuinely supports `high` visual density for standard image-driven long-form unless an explicit slow-burn exception applies.
- **Midpoint** — the middle changes what the story means, not merely what fact comes next.
- **Crisis** — an easy solution, assumption, or interpretation stops working.
- **Climax** — the proposed climax passes the three-question climax test in §7.6.
- **Payoff** — the final answer is earned by the climax rather than merely summarized.
- **Callback** — the opening image, question, or assumption has changed meaning by the end.

If any item cannot be answered concretely from the draft, the Script is not complete. Rewrite the affected section(s).

## Step 16 — Retention pass
Remove flat stretches, repeated conclusions, delayed obvious answers, detached lectures, and section transitions that advance only because topics are related.

Verify that:

- micro-payoffs are distributed;
- section endings create forward pressure;
- high-density sections do not stall visually;
- medium sections do not become lectures;
- low sections hold because the image/state is strong, not because nothing new was written.

## Step 17 — Mandatory rewrite/recheck loop
Run all applicable Story, Retention, Evidence, Visuality, Character, Spoken/TTS, Density, and Contract checks required by this skill and by the active task.

If any required pre-freeze check fails:

1. do **not** deliver;
2. do **not** freeze the Script yet;
3. rewrite the smallest affected section or transition that resolves the failure;
4. rerun every dependent gate that the rewrite could affect;
5. repeat until all required pre-freeze gates pass.

A Script is **complete** only after this loop passes.

## Step 18 — Freeze, then analyze when the task requires Script Analysis

When a structured task requires an exact frozen Script followed by Script Analysis:

1. freeze the Script only after Steps 14–17 pass;
2. after freezing, do not rewrite, paraphrase, normalize, or reorder the Script;
3. build Script Analysis only from the exact frozen text;
4. independently classify actual `pacing_density` using §14.5.5;
5. do not copy the internal planned density blindly into Analysis;
6. reconcile canonical semantic coverage and quote/range boundaries before delivery.

If a post-freeze analysis check reveals a problem that would require changing Script prose, the current frozen Script cannot be silently edited. Treat that as an authoring failure: return to pre-freeze authoring, revise, freeze a new final Script, then rebuild the analysis from scratch if the active task permits regeneration.

# 25. Scene Breakdown workflow

Before generating all beats, privately establish:

1. canonical protagonist(s);
2. canonical group(s);
3. canonical props;
4. canonical locations;
5. recurring visual subjects and exact meanings;
6. motifs and their planned evolution;
7. section-level lighting/time progression;
8. opening-to-climax callback;
9. which evidence sequences use graphic/explanatory visuals;
10. which people may appear incidentally without identity locks.

Then author beats.

Before authoring, also identify where narration contains independently drawable visual changes. Do not compress several genuine state changes into one broad illustration merely to reduce image count, and do not create micro-beats when the image state does not change.

Use a two-stage pacing discipline when the active task supplies `pacing_density`, section `planned_narration_wpm`, and a selected video pacing profile:

```text
semantic segmentation
→ semantic split/merge reconciliation
→ estimate timing with section WPM and compare rhythm with the selected profile band
→ semantic rescan only where rhythm is materially suspicious
→ final beat set
```

Do not consult `preferred_hold_seconds` to invent first-pass boundaries. After the semantic draft and merge-back exist, material mismatch with the selected profile requires conformance rescan, but the range remains non-mechanical: split or merge only for semantic reasons.

For every beat:

1. identify narrative change;
2. choose one dominant visual instant;
3. decide which canonical identities actually require consistency;
4. choose only refs that are semantically active;
5. write a beat-specific visual concept;
6. write drawable subject action;
7. choose framing based on story function;
8. choose camera angle based on meaning;
9. stage spatial relationships in composition;
10. preserve environment and lighting continuity;
11. verify the beat differs meaningfully from the previous beat.

---

# 26. Reference-selection checklist

Before finalizing each beat, ask:

### Character
- Is the protagonist actually visible?
- Is this exact recurring individual identity required?
- Is an unnamed helper being incorrectly promoted into a canonical character?
- If a group ref is selected, is the group genuinely the visual subject?

### Location
- Does exact environment identity matter in this frame?
- Or is contextual environment description enough?

### Prop
- Is the canonical object visible?
- Does recognition matter?
- Is this a callback or continuity-critical use?

### Recurring subject
- Does the selected subject exactly match what the visual depicts?
- Is left/right, injured/adapting, before/after, or active/inactive state correct?

### Motif
- Is the motif currently performing narrative work?
- Or is it merely adjacent to the beat?

Never select a ref “just in case.”

Every selected ref should have a reason.

---

# 27. Anti-boilerplate rules

Rewrite when any field could be pasted unchanged into many unrelated beats.

Watch for phrases such as:

- make the meaning clear;
- advance the scene;
- stage the decisive action;
- use spatial contrast;
- show the character's emotion;
- cinematic composition;
- visually communicate the idea;
- emphasize the narrative change.

These may be useful internal instructions.

They are weak beat content.

Every beat should become more specific after authoring, not merely longer.

---

# 28. Field ownership

When structured output contains multiple descriptive fields, prevent duplication by assigning each field one job.

Typical semantic separation:

```text
narrative role
= why this beat exists in the story

visual concept
= the memorable visual idea

subject action
= what is visibly happening now

environment emphasis
= contextual world details that matter

framing
= shot scale

camera angle
= viewpoint relative to subject

composition
= spatial arrangement and blocking

lighting
= motivated light direction/state

references
= canonical continuity locks
```

If two fields say the same sentence in different words, rewrite.

# 29. Preflight quality gates

Before delivering output, silently run these gates.

## Gate A — Story

- Is the dramatic question clear?
- Does each section change the story?
- For every adjacent major-section pair, can you complete: **“Because of what just happened, now we need to know/solve ___”** with a concrete causal answer?
- Is there escalation?
- Does evidence answer an active problem, change interpretation, create a consequence, or narrow uncertainty?
- Does the midpoint reinterpret?
- Does the crisis remove an easy solution or assumption?
- Does the climax pass all three questions in §7.6: accumulated test, new result, and final-answer impact?
- Would removing the proposed climax leave the resolution essentially unchanged? If yes, the climax fails.
- Does the ending pay off and transform the opening rather than merely summarize it?

## Gate B — Retention

- Does the opening move quickly from a concrete/familiar state to contradiction and a specific open question when appropriate?
- Does the first 30 seconds deliver the title/thumbnail promise?
- Is compelling material introduced early enough?
- Are micro-payoffs distributed?
- Are open loops remembered?
- Are there likely flat stretches?
- Are section endings forward-moving?

## Gate C — Character

- Is protagonist behavior consistent?
- Does pressure change behavior?
- Does the canonical protagonist preserve the visually meaningful identity promised by the title/thumbnail/Topic Idea?
- Have generic age comparisons, evidence subjects, or incidental people accidentally broadened or changed the protagonist identity?
- Are incidental people kept incidental?
- Are groups treated as groups?
- Are relationships staged, not merely stated?

## Gate D — Script visual-density integrity

When reviewing a Script before freeze:

- Was every major section given a deliberate planned density?
- Does the hook / cold open genuinely support high visual density for standard image-driven long-form?
- Does every planned `high` section naturally supply many independently drawable visual/story states?
- Are those states distributed across the section rather than concentrated in one small burst?
- Does any planned `high` section fake density through short rhetorical fragments that still resolve to the same image?
- Does every `medium` section provide regular visual progression with intentional absorption time?
- Does every `low` section contain fewer but strong states that can deliberately hold?
- Do several consecutive spoken blocks collapse into the same generic graphic, tooth, blank field, talking head, evidence plate, or repeated composition?
- If planned density and actual drawable-state density disagree, was the Script rewritten before freeze when the architecture required that density?
- Was the user-selected video pacing profile treated as a binding realized-density authoring target without becoming a beat quota, and was each section evaluated using its locked narration WPM?

FAIL when Scene Breakdown would have to invent or mechanically fragment visual beats to make the planned density believable.

## Gate E — Visual semantics and pacing reconciliation

- One still = one dominant time-state?
- Is any beat holding several independently drawable narration changes that should become separate visual states?
- Does any new beat merely restate the previous visual state without meaningful change?
- Is every visual concept beat-specific?
- Is subject action drawable?
- Does composition add spatial meaning?
- Are consecutive images meaningfully different?
- Does framing fit the number of subjects?
- Are camera choices motivated?
- Was the selected video pacing profile used only after semantic segmentation and rhetorical merge-back for Scene Breakdown conformance review?
- Did rhythm materially above the selected preferred range trigger a semantic rescan for missed independently drawable changes?
- Did rhythm materially below the selected preferred range trigger a merge-back rescan for fragments?
- Were all final splits or merges justified by meaning rather than by numeric compliance?

## Gate F — Reference semantics

- Every selected ref exists upstream?
- No invented canonical ID?
- Group refs only on real group shots?
- Props only when visible/continuity-critical?
- Locations selective rather than automatic?
- Recurring subjects exactly match current visual semantics?
- Motifs selected intentionally?

## Gate G — Evidence

- Documented / inferred / reconstructed levels remain distinct?
- Comparative evidence is not presented as the protagonist's direct record?
- No invented certainty?
- No visually overclaimed archaeological scene?

## Gate H — Contract

When a structured schema is provided:

- all required fields present;
- enum values valid;
- length constraints satisfied;
- no literal truncation;
- no silent schema expansion;
- script text preserved exactly when preservation is required.

Do not solve a schema problem by degrading creative meaning.

Rewrite concisely.

## Gate I — Structured analysis reconciliation

When the task includes Script Analysis or another structured semantic catalog, reconcile the analysis against the final/frozen source before delivery:

- Does every `pacing_density` label match the actual meaningful visual/story-state-change pattern of its final section?
- Are density labels derived from narration rather than `section_role`?
- For every canonical character, group, location, recurring prop, and recurring visual subject, is every clearly active narrative span covered by an appropriate semantic scope/range when the contract supports ranges?
- Are comparative, evidentiary, hypothetical-other, and reconstructed identities kept separate?
- Does every direct occurrence anchor lie inside the correct semantic scope?
- Are range boundaries exact, unique, ordered, non-overlapping, and no broader than the canonical entity's true narrative activity?

Presence/scope means downstream **eligibility**, not mandatory reference selection. Do not expand a range merely to force visual reuse.

## Gate J — Repetition

Check exact and semantic repetition in:

- narrative roles;
- visual concepts;
- composition;
- scene purposes;
- transition language;
- jokes;
- shot grammar.

Intentional callbacks are allowed.

Unintentional templates are not.

## Gate K — Spoken narration / TTS readiness

When the Script will be spoken or synthesized:

- Does the narration sound natural when heard, not merely polished when read?
- Are related short sentences bundled into coherent spoken blocks rather than isolated mechanically?
- Are short sentences used as emphasis rather than as the default cadence?
- Are long evidence sentences split when they overload the ear with clauses, numbers, dates, or caveats?
- Does sentence length vary intentionally?
- Does punctuation mark real meaning and prosodic boundaries?
- Are paragraph breaks reserved for major thought/story transitions rather than every dramatic fragment?
- Are exclamation marks and ellipses restrained?
- Are turn words positioned clearly enough for contrast and reversal to land?
- Does the climax answer land cleanly before qualification?
- Has all engine-specific markup been excluded unless the contract requests it?

FAIL when the Script would likely sound clipped, monotonous, breathless, syntactically ambiguous, overperformed, or essay-like when spoken.

## Mandatory gate remediation

These gates are not advisory. For every gate that applies to the requested artifact:

- a failed **pre-freeze** Story, Retention, Character, Spoken/TTS, Visual, Evidence, or Contract requirement requires revision before the Script/artifact may be considered complete;
- after revision, rerun the failed gate and every dependent gate;
- do not downgrade a structural failure to “good enough” merely because prose, research, or schema quality is otherwise strong;
- do not deliver while an applicable blocker remains.

For exact-source workflows, once the source Script is legitimately frozen, preserve it exactly. If fixing a discovered story failure requires changing frozen prose, regenerate from the authoring stage rather than silently mutating the frozen source during analysis.

# 30. Adversarial self-review

After normal QC, perform one final adversarial pass.

Ask:

- Where could a media model misread plurality?
- Where could it confuse left and right?
- Where could a generic person accidentally become a recurring character?
- Where could a location lock force repetitive backgrounds?
- Where could an unnecessary prop ref contaminate the image?
- Where does a still imply impossible chronology?
- Where does a group ref conflict with tight framing?
- Where does a historical image imply more certainty than the evidence supports?
- Which visual concept sounds like instruction instead of image?
- Which beat would look almost identical to the previous beat?
- Which callback fails to change meaning?

Fix genuine issues now.

Do not create new requirements merely because further polish is possible.

# 31. Blocker vs enhancement discipline

To avoid endless revision loops, classify findings.

## Blocker

Must fix before delivery:

- schema violation;
- missing or reordered required source text;
- central dramatic question is lost, materially changes without purpose, or is not resolved at the promised level;
- major sections do not form causal progression and advance mainly because they are related topics;
- a required climax is absent, is not caused by prior development, or fails the three-question climax test;
- evidence becomes a detached lecture that does not serve the active story problem for a materially long stretch;
- a prolonged flat stretch materially breaks the intended retention architecture;
- the ending fails to fulfill or transform the opening promise;
- invented canonical identity;
- wrong identity/group semantics;
- wrong recurring-subject meaning;
- materially incomplete canonical semantic scope/presence coverage that would prevent valid downstream continuity;
- contradictory continuity;
- unsupported factual certainty;
- impossible visual chronology;
- visual output that cannot fulfill the script beat.

## Quality issue

Should fix when materially beneficial:

- generic visual concept;
- weak staging;
- unnecessary ref;
- repeated composition;
- flat section ending;
- poorly motivated shot choice;
- redundant narration.

## Optional enhancement

Do not block release for:

- using every possible camera angle;
- hitting arbitrary ref-density targets;
- maximizing visual-concept lexical uniqueness;
- forcing extra motifs;
- adding complexity because it exists.

Do not turn optional variety into a quota.

# 32. Definition of done

A deliverable is ready when:

- the central promise is clear and fulfilled;
- story progression is causal and every major section has a defensible causal handoff;
- the climax passes the accumulated-test / new-result / answer-impact test;
- all applicable pre-freeze authoring gates passed before the Script was considered complete;
- evidence is integrated rather than dumped;
- characters behave consistently under pressure;
- title-promised protagonist identity remains semantically and visually consistent;
- narration is written for the ear, with coherent spoken blocks, intentional sentence-length variation, and meaningful punctuation;
- evidence-heavy sentences are orally parseable rather than overloaded;
- the final spoken-TTS pass has been completed before freeze when spoken narration is part of the task;
- every major Script section was intentionally planned for visual density before prose;
- the hook/cold open supports high visual density for standard image-driven long-form unless an explicit slow-burn exception applies;
- planned high/medium/low density is genuinely supported by the frozen narration rather than by rhetorical fragmentation;
- visual beats depict decisive, producible moments;
- the user-selected video pacing profile was used as a binding authoring/conformance target without turning timing into a beat quota;
- section narration WPM was planned and locked before prose, used for section-local timing estimates, and never replaced by a universal whole-video speaking rate;
- canonical identity semantics are correct;
- groups remain groups;
- incidental people remain incidental unless intentionally promoted upstream;
- recurring subjects match exactly;
- props and locations are selectively anchored;
- visual concepts are specific;
- composition adds spatial storytelling;
- camera grammar is semantically clean;
- intentional motifs pay off;
- schema/contract checks pass when applicable;
- final adversarial review finds no blocker.

Stop after this point.

Do not continue rewriting solely because another stylistic variation is possible.

---

# 33. Data-informed improvement after publication

When real YouTube retention data exists, use it to improve future authoring.

Review:

- **Intro retention** — did the first 30 seconds match the click promise and hold interest?
- **Top moments** — what conflict, reveal, image, joke, or explanation held viewers?
- **Spikes** — was the segment especially compelling, or was it unclear enough to require rewatching?
- **Dips** — did explanation become static, repetitive, predictable, or visually weak?
- **Typical retention comparison** — which structural choices outperform similar-length videos?

Convert observations into reusable authoring rules.

Do not overfit one video's curve.

Look for repeated patterns across videos.

# 34. Final creative standard

The final work should feel as though:

- a screenwriter shaped conflict and progression;
- a researcher protected evidence boundaries;
- a director staged spatial relationships;
- a cinematographer chose perspective for meaning;
- an editor protected momentum;
- a narrator could speak the prose naturally on the first read;
- a TTS system would receive clear syntactic and prosodic cues without theatrical punctuation clutter;
- an actor could perform behavior naturally;
- a comedian refined timing where appropriate;
- a continuity supervisor protected identity, props, injuries, and geography;
- a reviewer caught semantic mistakes before generation.

The objective is not maximum intensity.

The objective is controlled variation:

```text
curiosity
→ clarity
→ tension
→ effort
→ failure
→ understanding
→ adaptation
→ pressure
→ payoff
→ changed meaning
```

Every scene should give the viewer a reason to continue, and every visual decision should have a reason to exist.
